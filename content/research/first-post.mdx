## The question

Using one AI model for every part of a task is convenient, but it may not be
the most economical way to work. Different stages of a task place different
demands on a model. A short classification step may need speed and consistency,
while a difficult synthesis or review may benefit from a model with stronger
reasoning or a larger context window. This raises a practical research
question: can a task be split across models to reduce cost without reducing the
quality of the result?

## A possible approach

The basic idea is to route each subtask to the least expensive model that can
perform it reliably. A smaller model might extract fields, sort examples, or
draft a first pass. A more capable model could then handle ambiguous cases,
combine the intermediate work, or review the final answer. Some tasks may also
be checked by a separate model, a deterministic rule, or a person rather than
sent through the same expensive model again.

This is not simply a matter of assigning models by task label. The output of an
early stage affects the work that follows. A cheap extraction step that loses an
important detail can create errors that a later model cannot easily detect. A
useful routing system therefore needs explicit handoff formats, confidence
signals, and a way to escalate uncertain examples. It should also account for
the cost of retries, reviews, and longer prompts when comparing approaches.

## How to study it

An exploratory evaluation could begin with a small set of representative tasks
and a fixed quality rubric. Each task would be run through a single-model
baseline and several split workflows. The comparison would consider total token
and inference cost, completion time, error types, review effort, and the quality
of the final answer. It would be important to inspect failures by stage rather
than rely only on an overall score. A workflow that saves money by moving more
work to human reviewers may not be cheaper in practice.

The study should also test different routing thresholds. Sending every case to
the strongest model is unlikely to be necessary, while escalating too rarely
could produce silent quality loss. The useful operating point may differ by
task, domain, and consequence of error. Repeated runs would help separate a
stable pattern from an isolated result.

## Working hypothesis

The hypothesis is that model specialization can reduce cost when subtasks have
meaningfully different difficulty levels and when escalation is reliable. This
should be treated as a question to test, not as a shipped result or a promise of
equal quality. The next step is to define the task set, establish the baseline,
and measure where routing helps, where it fails, and what additional coordination
it requires.
